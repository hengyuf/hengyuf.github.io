Beyond the copy head: copy mechanisms at the output layer of a language model

A copy head lets a language model put probability on a token by pointing at an earlier position of the same document, inside the same softmax as the vocabulary. On a three-scale pretraining ladder of mixture-of-experts (MoE) transformer language models it lowers the loss at every scale. This page follows a line of work that tries to make it stronger: first a map of the literature, then several new base methods that each test one idea, then improved versions of the ones that work.

Every loss on this page is a final Paloma macro loss: the mean negative log-likelihood per token, in nats, on each of 16 evaluation sets from the public Paloma suite at the end of training, averaged over the sets with equal weight; lower is better. The three scales d512, d768 and d1024 are named by the hidden width of the model: 512, 768 and 1,024.

Score. score = 0.25 × gain(d512) + 0.25 × gain(d768) + 0.5 × gain(d1024), where gain(scale) = the plain baseline's loss − the method's loss at that scale, and each loss is the best (lowest) final Paloma macro loss over the three learning-rate multipliers 0.8 / 0.9 / 1.0. Higher is better; the plain baseline scores 0.

Goal and rules

The starting point: the copy head

Only the output layer changes. Let F_t be the final hidden state at position t, E_w the output embedding of token w, and K_i the attention keys that the last block has already computed for position i. A new projection W_Q turns the final state into one query per key head (the last block's keys come in heads of 128 dimensions). Every position i ≤ t of the same document, in every key head c, is a slot that stands for the token x_i found there.

  • l_w = F_t · E_w logit of the vocabulary token w
  • q_(t,c) = rms_norm(head c of F_t W_Q) query of position t
  • s_c(t,i) = g_c (q_(t,c) · K_(i,c)) / sqrt(128) + b score of slot i, with a learned gain g_c and bias b; 128 is the width of one key head
  • p(w | t) = (exp(l_w) + Σ over slots with x_i = w of exp(s)) / (Σ over w of exp(l_w) + Σ over all slots of exp(s))

The model's prediction is this one distribution: a single softmax over the vocabulary and all slots. The training loss and every evaluation number are its negative log-likelihood.

Rules

  • What is fixed. The MoE architecture of the trunk (the model below the output layer), the optimizer, the attention layout (full attention in some blocks, a sliding window in the others) and the training data do not change.
  • Official test. Nine training runs of one method: the three scales d512, d768 and d1024 (hidden width 512, 768 and 1,024; 6, 8 and 11 blocks), each at the learning-rate multipliers 0.8, 0.9 and 1.0, everything else fixed. A method is judged by the final Paloma macro loss at equal training steps (its definition and unit are under How to read this); lower is better.
  • Screening first. Before its official test a method is run once at the smallest scale and compared with the reference run and with a repeat of that run; only a lead that is clearly outside the run-to-run noise takes it on to the larger scales and then to the full nine-run test.
  • One idea per base method. A base method changes one thing. A stack of several methods is not a base method. Improved versions of a base method come afterwards and name the method they come from.
  • At most three at a time. No more than three methods of this page are under validation at the same time.
  • One proper distribution. The loss stays the negative log-likelihood of one normalised distribution over the vocabulary. No information about the token to be predicted, or about later tokens, may reach the prediction; every build is checked for such leaks before it is run.
  • Score. One number per method: the weighted sum of its gains over the plain baseline that is given at the top of this page. Half of the weight is on the largest scale, because the aim is a gain that holds as the model grows.
  • Not tied to one mechanism. A method need not reuse the keys of earlier layers or of any particular block; it only has to respect the rules above.

Literature map

The copy head belongs to a long line of mechanisms that let a language model reuse what its context already contains. The table sorts that line into families by three things that vary independently: where the stored content comes from (the document being read, or the training corpus), how it is addressed (an exact n-gram, or a learned key), and what comes back (a token id inside the output softmax, or a vector added to the hidden stream). The copy head is the combination: the document, a learned key of the last block, a token id.

Statements about a paper were checked against the paper's own arXiv page, and a number from a paper is quoted only where it was read there again. Mechanisms and relations are given in words. Sentences marked Reading are inferences of this log and have not been tested.

Three questions

Papers what the cited papers state Own runs what earlier runs on this ladder showed Reading an inference of this log, not a result Open not tested by anyone we found

1. How could the copy head use the keys of earlier layers, and not only the last layer's?

Papers We found no paper whose output pointer combines the keys of several layers; that is the result of a limited search, not a proof of absence. The two closest published heads each read one late state: Godey and Artzi 2026 (Copy-Paste) project the final hidden state into queries and keys, and Zhong, Lei and Chen 2022 take the input of the last feed-forward layer.

Indirect evidence says that the choice of state matters. In kNN-LM (Khandelwal et al. 2019) the best key among the states of the last layer was the input of its feed-forward network, better than the model's output; the authors suggest that the feed-forward layer serves the prediction while the attention layer represents the input. Xu, Alon and Neubig 2023 name a different input representation for the prediction as one of three reasons why kNN-LM works. Induction, the circuit that a copy mechanism imitates, is generally held to need at least two attention layers (Xu et al. 2024), and whole words are copied by other heads than single tokens (Feucht et al. 2025).

Own runs One multi-layer form was tried on this ladder before this line of work began: the keys of every full-attention layer as further sets of slots in the same softmax, each set with its own query. It did not improve on the last layer's keys alone. Keys projected from the final hidden state were also worse than the last block's attention keys.

Reading More sets of slots are more candidates, not more signal. What has not been tried is one slot per position whose score several layers decide together: a sum of per-layer scores, in which no layer has slots of its own, or a pointer read from a middle block while the decision when to copy stays at the top.

Open A slot score summed over depth; a pointer from a middle block; an early-layer query matched against the early-layer key of the preceding position, a learned and soft n-gram match; keys that read later positions instead of earlier layers (family F).

2. What is the relation to Engram (arXiv 2601.07372)?

Papers Engram (Cheng et al. 2026) is a trained table of n-gram embeddings. The last two or three input tokens are hashed to rows of the table, after a normalisation of token ids (case and similar variants) that shrinks a vocabulary of 128k by about 23 %. The retrieved vector is scaled by a gate computed from the current hidden state and added to the residual stream at a few chosen layers. The input embedding and the output layer stay as they are.

In the paper's sweep over insertion depths a single module works best at layer 2 and gets worse deeper. With the module switched off at inference, factual benchmarks keep 29 to 44 % of their score and reading-comprehension benchmarks 81 to 93 %: the authors conclude that the table holds knowledge about the corpus, while tasks grounded in the context rely on attention. They also report that the table and the MoE experts share one budget of sparse parameters, and that the best split gave the table about 20 to 25 % of it. The ladder of this page is an MoE.

Reading Engram and the copy head sit in opposite corners: a memory of the corpus, an exact n-gram address and a vector into early layers, against the tokens of the document, a learned contextual key and a token id at the output. They complement each other more than they compete: a copy head can only gain on tokens that recur in the document, a static table can serve the others.

What the copy head can borrow is the address, not the table. First, an address that costs the trunk no depth: the paper's analysis says that the table relieves the early layers of rebuilding local patterns, and a match-length feature or keys built from token identity would be the in-context counterpart. Second, surface variants: the normalisation of token ids is one of the three parts of Engram whose removal hurt most, and to a copy head a capitalised repeat of a word is a token that never occurred.

Open Match length or token-identity n-gram keys as a part of a slot's score; slots that also stand for the other spellings of their token; a static table in the first blocks, which would be a method of a class of its own because it changes the trunk.

3. What is the relation to test-time training for long context?

Papers Fast weights and attention are two readings of one memory. Ba et al. 2016 used fast weights as a way to attend to the recent past without storing copies of activity patterns. Sun et al. 2024 prove that a test-time-training layer with a linear inner model and batch gradient descent gives the same outputs as linear attention, and that with a kernel estimator as inner learner it gives self-attention. Wang, Shi and Fox 2025 derive linear attention, fast-weight programmers and softmax attention as special cases of one regression problem that differ in three choices: the regression weights, the function class and the optimisation algorithm.

Dynamic evaluation (Krause et al. 2017) adapts a model's weights by gradient steps on the text just read, which raises the probability of patterns that recur. Clark et al. 2022 write such gradient updates as linear attention and train the model with them, as a layer on top of a transformer. Tandon et al. 2025 let a sliding-window transformer keep learning at test time, so that it compresses the context into its weights at a cost per token that does not grow with the context.

Reading In these terms the copy head is the kernel form with token ids as values: every earlier position stays a slot of its own. A weight-matrix memory is a compressed version of the same slots. Its advantage is a state of constant size, which matters for very long contexts and buys nothing where the head already scores every position of the document.

What test-time training can still contribute is one of three changes: the write rule (write in proportion to the prediction error, as a gradient step does, or solve a least-squares problem, as von Oswald et al. 2025 do), the pooling of matches (a sum inside the exponent instead of a sum of exponentials), and the value space (an embedding, which can move probability to tokens not seen yet, instead of a token id).

Open A write strength per slot; a query whitened by the keys of the document; an error-corrected read in embedding space next to the slots.

Reading list

Grouped by topic. Each entry: authors, year of the first version, title, arXiv number, and one sentence on what the paper is cited for here.

Copy and pointer mechanisms at the output

  • Gu et al. (2016). Incorporating Copying Mechanism in Sequence-to-Sequence Learning. arXiv 1603.06393. CopyNet: a sequence-to-sequence decoder that can copy sub-sequences of its input next to generating words.
  • Merity et al. (2016). Pointer Sentinel Mixture Models. arXiv 1609.07843. Either reproduces a word of the recent context or produces one from the usual softmax.
  • Zhong, Lei and Chen (2022). Training Language Models with Memory Augmentation. arXiv 2205.12674. TRIME: trains with the memory in the loss; the probability is one softmax over the vocabulary and the memory entries.
  • Chang et al. (2023). Revisiting the Architectures like Pointer Networks to Efficiently Improve the Next Word Distribution, Summarization Factuality, and Beyond. arXiv 2305.12289. Pointer-like alternatives to the output softmax of transformer language models, motivated by the softmax bottleneck.
  • Godey and Artzi (2026). Copy-Paste Language Models. arXiv 2610.19247. A light copy head beside the usual output head, pretrained from 135 M to 1.7 B parameters: the closest published relative of the copy head of this page.
  • Godey and Artzi (2026). Lost in Backpropagation: The LM Head is a Gradient Bottleneck. arXiv 2603.10145. Argues that the output layer compresses the gradient that reaches the rest of the model, not only what the model can express.
  • Murugan (2026). Does the LM Head Create a Harmful Gradient Bottleneck? A Causal Test. arXiv 2608.16671. A causal test of that argument: it confirms the compression and does not find it established as harmful.
  • Yang et al. (2017). Breaking the Softmax Bottleneck: A High-Rank RNN Language Model. arXiv 1711.03953. The softmax bottleneck: one softmax over a low-rank product limits what a language model can express.

Caches and nearest-neighbour language models

  • Grave, Joulin and Usunier (2016). Improving Neural Language Models with a Continuous Cache. arXiv 1612.04426. Continuous cache: keeps past hidden states and reads them by a dot product with the current one; each stored state stands for the word that followed it.
  • Khandelwal et al. (2019). Generalization through Memorization: Nearest Neighbor Language Models. arXiv 1911.00172. kNN-LM: mixes a trained model with nearest neighbours from a datastore of pairs (representation of a context, the word that followed), without further training.
  • Xu, Alon and Neubig (2023). Why do Nearest Neighbor Language Models Work? arXiv 2301.02828. Why kNN-LM works: a different input representation for the prediction, approximate search, and the temperature of the softmax.
  • Alon et al. (2022). Neuro-Symbolic Language Modeling with Automaton-augmented Retrieval. arXiv 2201.12431. Pointers between consecutive datastore entries turn the datastore into an automaton that is followed during inference.
  • Wu et al. (2022). Memorizing Transformers. arXiv 2203.08913. An approximate nearest-neighbour lookup into a memory of recent (key, value) pairs that is not trained through.

Induction heads and in-context n-grams

  • Olsson et al. (2022). In-context Learning and Induction Heads. arXiv 2209.11895. Induction heads complete a pattern A B ... A with B; evidence that they carry much of in-context learning.
  • Edelman et al. (2024). The Evolution of Statistical Induction Heads: In-Context Learning Markov Chains. arXiv 2402.11004. Transformers trained on Markov chains form statistical induction heads; training passes from a unigram solution to the bigram solution, and the simpler solution may delay the better one.
  • Varre, Yüce and Flammarion (2025). Learning In-context n-grams with Transformers: Sub-n-grams Are Near-stationary Points. arXiv 2508.12837. Solutions that use a shorter context than the data needs are near-stationary points of the loss, which explains plateaus and learning in stages.
  • D'Angelo et al. (2026). Induction Heads Interpolate N-Grams. arXiv 2607.02800. Induction heads as smoothed n-gram estimators: exact and partial matches of the context are weighted by their overlap.
  • Akyürek et al. (2024). In-Context Language Learning: Architectures and Algorithms. arXiv 2401.12973. n-gram heads, higher-order variants of induction heads; building them into models also improved natural language modelling.
  • Xu et al. (2024). KV Shifting Attention Enhances Language Modeling. arXiv 2411.19574. KV shifting attention: each key and value becomes a learned blend with its neighbour's, which lowers the depth and width the induction mechanism needs.
  • Feucht et al. (2025). The Dual-Route Model of Induction. arXiv 2504.03022. Concept-level induction heads copy whole words and attend to the ends of multi-token words, beside token-level induction heads.
  • McDougall et al. (2023). Copy Suppression: Comprehensively Understanding an Attention Head. arXiv 2310.04625. Copy suppression: a head that lowers the probability of a token which earlier layers predict and which occurs in the context.
  • Zhang et al. (2024). Memory Mosaics. arXiv 2405.06394. Networks of associative memories with in-context learning abilities, compared with transformers on medium-scale language modelling.

Engram and other n-gram memories

  • Cheng et al. (2026). Conditional Memory via Scalable Lookup: A New Axis of Sparsity for Large Language Models. arXiv 2601.07372. Engram: a hashed table of n-gram embeddings, gated by the hidden state and added in a few layers; presented as a second kind of sparsity next to MoE.
  • Yang et al. (2026). FactorEngram: Factorized N-gram Memory with Basis-Level Gating for Language Models. arXiv 2609.35578. n-gram memory as coefficients over a shared dictionary of basis vectors, gated per component.
  • Zhou et al. (2026). Tensorizing Engram: Sharing Latents Across N-Gram Embeddings is Beneficial in LLMs. arXiv 2606.08347. n-gram embeddings in tensor form, with factors shared across n-gram orders.
  • Roy et al. (2022). N-Grammer: Augmenting Transformers with latent n-grams. arXiv 2207.06366. Augments a transformer with n-grams built from a discrete latent representation of the text.
  • Huang et al. (2025). Over-Tokenized Transformer: Vocabulary is Generally Worth Scaling. arXiv 2501.16975. A much larger input vocabulary of multi-gram tokens, decoupled from the output vocabulary.
  • Yu et al. (2025). Scaling Embedding Layers in Language Models. arXiv 2502.01637. Embeddings for frequent n-grams, learned by a separate model and kept off the accelerator.
  • Liu et al. (2024). Infini-gram: Scaling Unbounded n-gram Language Models to a Trillion Tokens. arXiv 2401.17377. A count-based model with contexts of unbounded length and backoff; combined with neural models it lowers their perplexity.
  • Li et al. (2022). N-gram Is Back: Residual Learning of Neural Text Generation with n-gram Language Model. arXiv 2210.14431. A neural model that learns the residual between an n-gram model and the data.
  • Lample et al. (2019). Large Memory Layers with Product Keys. arXiv 1907.05242. Product-key memory: a very large trained key-value memory layer with exact nearest-neighbour search.
  • Berges et al. (2024). Memory Layers at Scale. arXiv 2412.09764. Trained key-value memory layers at scale; the gains are largest on factual tasks.

Fast weights and test-time training

  • Ba et al. (2016). Using Fast Weights to Attend to the Recent Past. arXiv 1610.06258. Fast weights store temporary memories of the recent past and implement a kind of attention to it.
  • Krause et al. (2017). Dynamic Evaluation of Neural Sequence Models. arXiv 1709.07432. Dynamic evaluation: the model is adapted to the recent history by gradient steps, which raises the probability of patterns that recur.
  • Rae et al. (2018). Fast Parametric Learning with Activation Memorization. arXiv 1803.10049. Treats a subset of the model's parameters as a fast memory, to bind rare classes quickly.
  • Clark et al. (2022). Meta-Learning Fast Weight Language Models. arXiv 2212.02475. Fast weight layers: the gradient updates of dynamic evaluation written as linear attention and used during training too.
  • Sun et al. (2024). Learning to (Learn at Test Time): RNNs with Expressive Hidden States. arXiv 2407.04620. Layers whose hidden state is a small model trained on the sequence while it is read; with a linear model and batch gradient descent this equals linear attention.
  • Behrouz, Zhong and Mirrokni (2024). Titans: Learning to Memorize at Test Time. arXiv 2501.00663. A neural long-term memory module that learns to memorise at test time, beside attention as short-term memory.
  • Tandon et al. (2025). End-to-End Test-Time Training for Long Context. arXiv 2512.23675. A sliding-window transformer keeps learning on the context at test time and so compresses it into its weights.
  • Wang, Shi and Fox (2025). Test-time regression: a unifying framework for designing sequence models with associative memory. arXiv 2501.12352. Linear attention, fast-weight programmers and softmax attention as special cases of one regression problem solved at test time.
  • von Oswald et al. (2025). MesaNet: Sequence Modeling by Locally Optimal Test-Time Training. arXiv 2506.05233. A layer that minimises an in-context regression loss to optimality at every step.
  • Feng et al. (2026). In-Place Test-Time Training. arXiv 2604.06169. The last projection of the MLP blocks as fast weights, with an objective aligned with next-token prediction.

Multi-token prediction, spans and look-ahead

  • Gloeckle et al. (2024). Better & Faster Large Language Models via Multi-token Prediction. arXiv 2404.19737. Predicts several future tokens with separate heads on a shared trunk, as an auxiliary task; on small algorithmic tasks it favours the formation of induction heads.
  • DeepSeek-AI et al. (2024). DeepSeek-V3 Technical Report. arXiv 2412.19437. A large MoE model trained with a multi-token prediction objective.
  • Zuhri, Fuadi and Aji (2025). Predicting the Order of Upcoming Tokens Improves Language Modeling. arXiv 2508.19228. Finds multi-token prediction inconsistent as an auxiliary loss and predicts the order of upcoming tokens instead.
  • Mahajan et al. (2025). Beyond Multi-Token Prediction: Pretraining LLMs with Future Summaries. arXiv 2510.14751. An auxiliary head predicts a summary of the long-term future instead of exact tokens.
  • Song et al. (2025). Causal Attention with Lookahead Keys. arXiv 2509.07301. Keys of earlier positions are updated with the tokens that follow them, without breaking the autoregressive property.
  • Panthaplackel, Allamanis and Brockschmidt (2020). Copy that! Editing Sequences by Copying Spans. arXiv 2006.04771. Copies whole spans of the input in one step, with a training objective for the many ways to produce the same output.
  • Singh et al. (2020). CopyNext: Explicit Span Copying and Alignment in Sequence to Sequence Models. arXiv 2010.15266. An explicit token-level copy operation extended to spans, with hard alignments.
  • Lan et al. (2023). Copy Is All You Need. arXiv 2307.06962. Text generation as copying of text segments from a collection instead of choosing words from a vocabulary.

Base methods and their status

A base method changes one thing about the copy head. An evolved method improves a base method and names it. The short code after a method's name is its tag, the label under which its runs are kept. The status of each method is one of the words explained at the end of the page.

The eight methods below are what the first design round left. Seven are base methods in the sense above, each a single idea. The eighth adds a module to the trunk and leaves the copy head as it is, so it is not a change of the copy head; it is listed because the relation to Engram is one of the starting questions. The first six are to be screened alone against the copy-head reference; the last two need a decision before they are built. None of the eight has a result yet. Prior expectations are modest: the earlier changes of the head's form that were checked against this reference did not end ahead of it by more than the run-to-run noise.

Each panel gives the definition of its method and names its starting question: one of the three questions of the literature map (the keys of earlier layers, the relation to Engram, the relation to test-time training) or, for two methods, n-grams and spans, which the map treats in its table of families. A note at the top of the panel says where the method stands, more finely than the status word “designed”, which all eight carry: “first wave: being built”, “queued”, or “needs a decision before it is built”.

1Variant slotscphcb4designed–

A slot may also return the other surface forms of its token (another case, with or without a leading space) at a lower, learned score, and the query says which form is due now.

base methodclass: output layer onlydesigned since 2026-11-18

first wave: being built

What changes relative to the copy head

In the copy head a slot can only return the token that stands at its position, so a repeat of a word in another case or with other spacing finds no slot. Here a fixed table sorts token ids into classes of the same letters. A token belongs to the table if its text, without the mark of a leading space, consists of ASCII letters only; tokens whose letters are equal once they are lower-cased form a class if there are at least two of them. Each member has one of six forms: with or without a leading space, and lower case, capitalised (only the first letter in upper case) or other.

A slot gives its probability mass to its own token as before and, lowered by an offset, to the other members of its token's class (its variant returns). The offset depends on the form of the returned token and on the query, so the final hidden state can ask for a capital after a full stop or for no space after a quotation mark. Everything else is the copy head. The prediction stays one softmax, over the vocabulary, the slots and the slots' variant returns, and the loss stays the negative log-likelihood of this one distribution.

Formulas
  • form(w) = the form of token w in the table, one of six (f = 1 … 6)
  • d_f(t) = min(−4 + a_f + (F_t · u_f) / sqrt(D), 20) offset of form f for the query at position t; a_f and u_f are learned and start at 0; D is the hidden width
  • mass(w | t) = exp(l_w) + Σ over slots with x_i = w of exp(s) + exp(d_f(t)) · Σ over slots whose token is another member of the class of w of exp(s), with f = form(w)
  • p(w | t) = mass(w | t) / Σ over all tokens v of mass(v | t)
  • s is the slot score s_c(t,i) of the copy head; a token that is in no class has no third term
Why it could help

Starting question: the relation to Engram (question 2). Engram normalises token ids (case and similar variants) before it looks up its table, and that normalisation is one of the three parts whose removal hurt most in the paper (Cheng et al. 2026). To a copy head a capitalised repeat of a word is a token that never occurred. This method applies the normalisation to what a slot returns, not to the address of a table.

What it costs

Six offset vectors of the hidden width and six scalars: 3,078, 4,614 and 6,150 new parameters at the three scales. The table is fixed and holds nothing that is trained.

Labels and caveats
  • The table is made by a fixed rule from the tokenizer's vocabulary alone; no statistic of the training or evaluation data enters it. It is a prior chosen by hand.
  • At the first training step the model is not exactly the copy head: with the offset at −4, every slot already gives each other member of its token's class exp(−4), about 2 %, of the weight it gives its own token.
2Look-ahead keyscphfb4designed–

A slot still stands for its own token, but its score also reads the key of the next position, the first state that has seen what followed that token; no future token is used, because this key is read only for slots before the current position.

base methodclass: output layer onlydesigned since 2026-11-18

first wave: being built

What changes relative to the copy head

The key of a slot is computed before the next token exists, so it cannot say how the text went on after the slot's token. Here the score of slot i gets a second term: a second query, from a second new projection of the final hidden state, matched against the last block's key of position i + 1. Slot i still returns the token x_i, and everything else is the copy head.

The method does not use future tokens. The key of position i + 1 depends only on the tokens up to position i + 1, and it is read only for slots i < t, for which i + 1 ≤ t is a position the model has already read. The slot of the current position, i = t, has no second term, and position i + 1 always lies in the same document as the slot. The prediction stays one softmax over the vocabulary and the slots, and the loss stays the negative log-likelihood of this one distribution.

Formulas
  • q'_(t,c) = rms_norm(head c of F_t W'_Q) a second query, from a second new projection W'_Q
  • s_c(t,i) = g_c (q_(t,c) · K_(i,c)) / sqrt(128) + b + [i < t] g'_c (q'_(t,c) · K_(i+1,c)) / sqrt(128)
  • [i < t] = 1 for a slot before the current position, 0 for the slot i = t; the new gain g'_c starts at 0, so the model starts as the copy head
  • p(w | t) as in the copy head, with these scores
Why it could help

Starting question: n-grams and spans (families E and F of the literature map). When the token to be predicted begins a word of several tokens, the final hidden state may encode the whole word more sharply than the word's first token. The state one position later has seen how the slot's token went on, so its key can mark the slots that began that word. Keys that are updated with the tokens after their position are used inside the trunk by Song et al. 2025, and whole words are copied by heads that attend to the ends of multi-token words (Feucht et al. 2025).

What it costs

A second query projection and one gain per key head: 65,537, 98,305 and 262,146 new parameters at the three scales. One more product of queries and keys per slot.

3Depth-summed scorecphdb4designed–

One slot per position, as in the copy head, whose score is the sum of one query-key match in the key space of every full-attention layer, instead of the last layer's match alone.

base methodclass: output layer onlydesigned since 2026-11-18

first wave: being built

What changes relative to the copy head

The copy head reads the keys of the last block only. Here every full-attention layer below the last block hands its attention keys to the output layer as well: one such layer at the two smaller scales, two at the largest (the last block is itself a full-attention layer at every scale). Each of these layers gets a query projection and a gain of its own, and its match is added to the score of the same slot. There is still one slot per position and key head.

The form that was tried before gave each layer a set of slots of its own inside the softmax (see question 1), so the sets stood side by side as rival candidates. In a sum no layer has slots of its own, and a layer whose gain stays at zero changes nothing. The prediction stays one softmax over the vocabulary and the slots, and the loss stays the negative log-likelihood of this one distribution.

Formulas
  • q^l_(t,c) = rms_norm(head c of F_t W^l_Q) one new query projection W^l_Q for each earlier full-attention layer l
  • s_c(t,i) = g_c (q_(t,c) · K_(i,c)) / sqrt(128) + b + Σ over the earlier full-attention layers l of g^l_c (q^l_(t,c) · K^l_(i,c)) / sqrt(128)
  • K^l = the attention keys of layer l; the new gains g^l_c start at 0, so the model starts as the copy head
  • p(w | t) as in the copy head, with these scores
Why it could help

Starting question: the keys of earlier layers (question 1). No paper was found whose output pointer combines the keys of several layers. Keys of different depths may hold different things about a position; a sum lets the layers add to one decision instead of competing for it. Whether their keys complement each other or are substitutes is what the method tests.

What it costs

One query projection and one gain per key head for each earlier full-attention layer: 65,537, 98,305 and 524,292 new parameters at the three scales. One more product of queries and keys per slot for each such layer.

Labels and caveats
  • It reads the full-attention layers only. The keys of the sliding-window blocks, among them the first blocks of the model, are not part of it.
4Exact-match keycphib4designed–

A slot's score gets learned bonuses when the last one, two or three tokens of the context are exactly the tokens that precede the slot, so that the exact n-gram address of induction costs the trunk nothing.

base methodclass: output layer onlydesigned since 2026-11-18

queued

What changes relative to the copy head

Induction looks for the earlier places where the current token occurred and copies what followed them. The copy head has to learn this address through its keys. Here it is given as three exact features of a slot: the current token equals the token just before the slot; the last two tokens equal the two before the slot; the last three equal the three before it. Each feature that holds adds a learned bonus to the slot's score. Everything else is the copy head.

The features compare tokens the model has already read, the current token and its predecessors with the tokens before the slot; they never compare the token to be predicted with the slot's token, and no comparison crosses the start of a document. The prediction stays one softmax over the vocabulary and the slots, and the loss stays the negative log-likelihood of this one distribution.

Formulas
  • e_1(t,i) = [x_t = x_(i−1)], e_2(t,i) = e_1(t,i) · [x_(t−1) = x_(i−2)], e_3(t,i) = e_2(t,i) · [x_(t−2) = x_(i−3)]
  • […] = 1 if the two tokens are equal and both positions lie inside the document, else 0
  • s_c(t,i) = g_c (q_(t,c) · K_(i,c)) / sqrt(128) + b + β_(c,1) e_1(t,i) + β_(c,2) e_2(t,i) + β_(c,3) e_3(t,i)
  • the bonuses β start at 0, so the model starts as the copy head; p(w | t) as in the copy head, with these scores
Why it could help

Starting question: the relation to Engram (question 2). Engram addresses its table by the exact identity of the last two or three input tokens, and the paper's analysis says that the table relieves the early layers of rebuilding local patterns (Cheng et al. 2026). An exact match of the last tokens is the same kind of address inside the document. The method tests whether an address that costs no depth helps the copy head or relieves the trunk.

What it costs

Three scalars per key head: 3, 3 and 6 new parameters at the three scales. Three comparisons of token ids for every pair of a query and a slot.

5Test-time query adaptationcphyb4designed–

Before a position predicts, its query is corrected by one gradient step, taken in closed form, on the copy errors that the head made at the earlier positions of the document, whose next tokens are known by then.

base methodclass: output layer onlydesigned since 2026-11-18

queued

What changes relative to the copy head

At every earlier position j of the document the copy head pointed somewhere, and by the time position t is reached the token that came next, x_(j+1), is known. If that token had a slot, the difference between where the pointer should have looked and where it looked is an error key: up to a factor it is the gradient, with respect to the query of position j, of the logarithm of the share of the pointer's weight that lay on the slots of that token. Let the correction be a linear map W of the query, q → q + W q, with W = 0 at the start of the document. One gradient step on W over these earlier positions makes W proportional to the sum of the outer products of each error key with its query; applied to the present query this gives the correction Δ of the formulas: a mean of the error keys, each weighted by how similar its query is to the present one. The slot scores are those of the corrected query q + ρ Δ, with a learned step size ρ.

Only tokens up to the current one are used: the error key of position j needs x_(j+1), and j < t. In training the correction is treated as a constant, so no gradient flows through the first pass. The prediction stays one softmax over the vocabulary and the slots, and the loss stays the negative log-likelihood of this one distribution.

Formulas
  • π_j = softmax of the copy-head scores s_c(j,i) over all slots of position j (positions i ≤ j and key heads c together)
  • π+_j = the same softmax over the slots that hold the token that came next, x_i = x_(j+1); a position j without such a slot is left out
  • e_(j,c) = Σ over i of (π+_j(i,c) − π_j(i,c)) K_(i,c) error key of position j
  • Δ_(t,c) = (1 / n_t) Σ over the earlier positions j < t of the document that have an error key of e_(j,c) (q_(j,c) · q_(t,c)) / 128; n_t = the number of these positions, Δ = 0 if there is none
  • s_c(t,i) = g_c (q_(t,c) · K_(i,c)) / sqrt(128) + b + ρ_c g_c (Δ_(t,c) · K_(i,c)) / sqrt(128)
  • the step size ρ_c starts at 0, so the model starts as the copy head; p(w | t) as in the copy head, with these scores
Why it could help

Starting question: the relation to test-time training (question 3). The copy head already keeps every earlier position as a slot of its own, which a memory in fast weights can only compress. What test-time training does and the copy head does not is to learn from the errors made on the text just read, as dynamic evaluation does with the weights of a model (Krause et al. 2017). This method takes one such step, on the query alone.

What it costs

One learned step size per key head: 1, 1 and 2 new parameters at the three scales. A second pass over the scores of the earlier positions of the document, without gradient.

Labels and caveats
  • The mathematics of this definition gets one more independent check before the method is built; a formula may still change.
6Pair-novelty flagcphnb4designed–

Each slot carries one exact fact, whether the pair made of the token before it and its own token occurred there for the first time in the document, and the query sets how much such slots count, so that a token can be counted once per distinct predecessor instead of once per occurrence.

base methodclass: output layer onlydesigned since 2026-11-18

queued

What changes relative to the copy head

A flag marks the slots whose pair (the token before the slot, the slot's token) had not occurred earlier in the document; the first position of a document is marked too. The flag is computed from token ids alone. A weight for the marked slots, read from the final hidden state, is added to their score: with a positive weight the pointer leans towards the first time a token followed each of its predecessors, with a negative weight towards repeats of a pair. Everything else is the copy head.

The flag of slot i depends only on the tokens up to position i. The prediction stays one softmax over the vocabulary and the slots, and the loss stays the negative log-likelihood of this one distribution.

Formulas
  • ν_i = 1 if i is the first position of its document, or if no earlier position j of the document has x_(j−1) = x_(i−1) and x_j = x_i; else 0
  • a(t) = α + (F_t · u) / sqrt(D) weight of the flag for the query at position t; α and u are learned and start at 0, so the model starts as the copy head; D is the hidden width
  • s_c(t,i) = g_c (q_(t,c) · K_(i,c)) / sqrt(128) + b + a(t) ν_i
  • p(w | t) as in the copy head, with these scores
Why it could help

Starting question: n-grams and spans (families D and H of the literature map). Counting a token once for every distinct token it has followed, instead of once per occurrence, is the continuation count of Kneser-Ney smoothing for n-gram models: a token that has followed many different tokens is more likely to follow a new one. The copy head sums over occurrences. The flag lets the query choose between the two ways of counting.

What it costs

One vector of the hidden width and one scalar: 513, 769 and 1,025 new parameters at the three scales.

7Slots from earlier documentscphrb4designed–

The positions of the earlier documents in the same input sequence become slots too, with a gain and a bias of their own; the trunk is unchanged and still sees only the current document.

base methodclass: output layer; reads earlier documents of the sequence (a class of its own)designed since 2026-11-18

needs a decision before it is built

What changes relative to the copy head

An input sequence can hold several documents one after another. The attention of the trunk and the copy head stay inside the document of the current position. Here every earlier position of the whole sequence is a slot: a slot of the current document is scored as in the copy head, a slot of an earlier document with a gain and a bias of its own, which start low. Only the output layer can copy across documents; the trunk is not changed.

Earlier documents stand earlier in the sequence, so no later token is used. The prediction stays one softmax, over the vocabulary and both kinds of slots, and the loss stays the negative log-likelihood of this one distribution. What changes is what the prediction is conditioned on (see the caveats).

Formulas
  • slots = every position i ≤ t of the input sequence, in every key head c (in the copy head: of the document of position t)
  • s_c(t,i) = g_c (q_(t,c) · K_(i,c)) / sqrt(128) + b for a slot in the document of position t
  • s_c(t,i) = g'_c (q_(t,c) · K_(i,c)) / sqrt(128) + b' − 6 for a slot in an earlier document; g'_c and b' are learned and start at 0
  • p(w | t) as in the copy head, over both kinds of slots
Why it could help

Starting question: the relation to test-time training for long context (question 3). What such training promises is a memory of text that attention no longer reaches. On this ladder the only such text is the earlier documents of the same input sequence.

What it costs

One gain per key head and one bias: 2, 2 and 3 new parameters at the three scales.

Labels and caveats
  • It needs a decision before it is built. Every loss of such a model is a loss given the earlier documents of its evaluation sequence, so it depends on how the evaluation text is cut and ordered; no other number on this page does. Whether the rules of this page allow that, and whether such a row may stand in the results table, is not decided.
  • If it is allowed, it is a class of its own and its results are labelled so.
8Engram table before the second blockcpheb4designed–

A port of Engram onto the model with the copy head: a trained table, addressed by hashes of the last two and of the last three input tokens, adds a gated vector to the hidden stream in front of the second block.

base methodclass: changes the trunk (a class of its own)designed since 2026-11-18

needs a decision before it is built

What changes relative to the copy head

At every position the last two and the last three input tokens are each hashed, by four hash functions, to rows of eight trained tables. The eight rows are joined, scaled by a gate that is computed from the hidden state, and added to the hidden stream in front of the second block. The address reads the current token and the two before it, nothing later. The output layer is the copy head, unchanged, so the prediction stays one softmax over the vocabulary and the slots, and the loss stays the negative log-likelihood of this one distribution.

Formulas
  • z_(t,n,k) = hash_(n,k)(x_(t−n+1), …, x_t) mod p_(n,k) n = 2, 3; four hash functions k for each n; the eight table sizes p_(n,k) are distinct primes near 262,144
  • e_t = the eight rows T_(n,k)[z_(t,n,k)] of the trained tables T_(n,k), 32 numbers each, joined to 256 numbers
  • α_t = sigmoid(rms_norm(h_t) · rms_norm(e_t W_K) / sqrt(D)) gate; h_t is the hidden state in front of the second block, D the hidden width; W_K and W_V are trained projections from the 256 numbers to the hidden width
  • h_t ← h_t + γ α_t (e_t W_V) γ starts at 0, so the model starts as the copy head
  • output layer: the copy head, unchanged
Why it could help

Starting question: the relation to Engram (question 2). This is the module itself, not a transfer of one of its parts. A copy mechanism can only help on tokens that already occurred in the document; a table trained on the whole corpus aims at the others.

What it costs

Eight tables of about 262,144 rows of 32 numbers each, about 67 million new parameters at every scale, and two projections.

Labels and caveats
  • It needs a decision before it is built. It does not change the copy head: it adds a module and about 67 million parameters to the trunk, which the rules of this page keep fixed. A gain would be the table's own and would say little about copying.
  • If it is run, it needs two controls: the same table with shuffled addresses, and a baseline with the same number of added parameters.
  • A class of its own: it changes the trunk.

How to read this

Loss

The final Paloma macro loss. At the end of training the model is evaluated on 16 evaluation sets from the public Paloma suite. The loss of a set is the mean negative log-likelihood per token of the model's one output distribution, with the natural logarithm: its unit is nats per token. The macro loss is the mean of the 16 per-set losses with equal weight. Lower is better. A gain is a difference of two such losses and a score is a weighted mean of gains, so both are in nats per token too.

Scales

d512, d768 and d1024 are the three model sizes of the ladder, named by their hidden width: 512, 768 and 1,024. They have 6, 8 and 11 blocks.

Official test

Nine training runs of one method: the three scales, each at the three learning-rate multipliers, everything else fixed. The loss of a method at a scale is the best (lowest) of its three runs there. All comparisons are at equal training steps.

Score

A weighted sum of the gains over the plain baseline, with half of the weight on the largest scale: the aim is a gain that holds as the model grows. The plain baseline scores 0 by definition.

Row to beat

The copy head on the plain baseline, tagged cphb4. The column “Score − copy head” says how far a method is above or below it.

Gain

The plain baseline's loss minus the method's loss at the same scale. Gains are computed from unrounded losses, so a gain can differ from the difference of the two printed losses by one unit in the last digit.

Noise

Repeated runs on this ladder say that a score difference below about 0.002 is not meaningful, and that a single gain at d512 or d1024 is uncertain by 0.002 to 0.003.

Step time

Given as a ratio only: the method's time for one optimizer step divided by the plain baseline's at the same scale. Scores are at equal training steps, not at equal time. The copy head on the plain baseline has step-time ratios 1.026, 1.004 and 1.008 at the three scales.

Class

Says what a method changes, so that like is compared with like. A method that changes only the output layer is in the class of the copy head. A method that adds a loss term, changes the trunk or needs an extra artefact built from the training data is a class of its own and is labelled so.

Evidence tags

In the literature map, Papers marks what the cited papers state, Own runs what earlier runs on this ladder showed, Reading an inference of this log that has not been tested, and Open what nobody we found has tested.
